Your screen, through the day.

Daylight shifts your display's warmth and brightness on a schedule you set — gradually, predictably, and out of the way. It lives in the menu bar, works entirely offline, and tells you exactly what it is doing and why.

It tells you what it is doing

Most of the work in Daylight went into being explainable. Every state has a sentence attached, in plain language, naming whatever is responsible for it.

Switch layers on and watch them argue

The highest layer that speaks about a control wins it — and warmth and brightness are decided separately, so a rule that only dims cannot quietly discard your schedule's warmth. The six layers and the order below are the app's own; the little schedules and rules attached to them are examples, so there is something for the resolver to decide between.

Top of the ladder — overrules everything below

    The base layer, if nothing above it speaks

    That sentence is not written for this page. It comes from the same resolver the app runs, checked against it across every combination of the six layers.

    A ladder, not a pile of rules

    Six layers, a fixed order, no user-assignable priorities. A rules system you can predict beats one you can fully rewire, and the ladder is printed in the app with the active layer marked — the same one you just used above.

    Three levels of certainty

    Daylight distinguishes what it asked for, what macOS accepted, and what it could read back and confirm. Each display reports which of the three applies. An API returning success is not evidence that anything changed, and the app does not treat it as such.

    It notices the competition

    f.lux, Night Shift and similar tools write to the same colour table. Daylight reads back after every write, and when something else has overwritten it, it says so rather than silently losing. It will not fight another app, and will not disable one behind your back.

    The details that took the longest

    Schedules that survive real life

    Daylight-saving changes, time-zone moves, clocks corrected by hand, schedules that cross midnight, night shifts, non-Western weeks, polar day and polar night. Anchors follow the clock; fades follow elapsed time. Getting that backwards put the whole schedule an hour out on a 23-hour day — a test now pins it.

    Sunrise without a permission prompt

    Solar times are computed on your Mac from a place you choose — 101 cities built in, or coordinates. Daylight can suggest a starting point from your time zone, which is a setting rather than a position. It never asks macOS where you are and makes no network requests.

    Displays remembered properly

    Settings key to a durable identity, never a display index — indices are reassigned on every dock and wake. Two identical monitors that genuinely cannot be told apart are reported as ambiguous instead of guessed at.

    Fades paced by perception

    Interpolation runs in mired rather than kelvin, because 2000→3000 K is a large visible change and 8000→9000 K is nearly invisible. The write rate is derived from how much the value actually moves, so a 90-minute ramp writes about once a minute instead of once a frame.

    It refuses a suspicious baseline

    Before adjusting anything, Daylight captures what "normal" means for a display — and validates it. When f.lux was running during development the table read back as R 1.00 / G 0.36 / B 0.10; adopting that as neutral would have baked another app's tint in permanently. There is a test pinned to those exact numbers.

    Getting out of the way

    ⇧⌥⌘R restores normal output from anywhere, with no permissions required. Quitting restores your displays. If the app is force quit or crashes, macOS restores the colour table itself — verified under SIGKILL, which is why Daylight ships no watchdog helper.

    Most days, you never open the window

    The menu bar is where the app actually lives. Warmth and brightness, every mode, a pause with a duration, and a restore — all one click from the icon. On a Mac with more than one display, a selector decides which of them a change applies to.

    • The icon fills in proportion to how warm the current setting is, and shows paused and off as distinct shapes rather than colours — so it reads at a glance, and reads at all if you cannot tell those colours apart.
    • A pause has a duration. Fifteen minutes, an hour, until the next scheduled change, or until you say otherwise — and it tells you when it ends.
    • Restore is always one click away, and ⇧⌥⌘R does it from anywhere without Daylight needing any permission at all.

    What it doesn't claim

    This is a comfort and routine tool. It is not a medical one, and the wording throughout the app is careful about the difference.

    • It does not prevent eye damage, treat eye strain, or improve your sleep.
    • There are no invented "circadian", "eye health" or "melatonin" scores. There is no score of any kind.
    • Temperature values are approximate display targets, not measurements of emitted light.
    • What it calls brightness is software dimming — it lowers the values sent to the panel. It is not backlight control, and macOS gives applications no public way to change a backlight. The app says so wherever the word appears.
    • It does not calibrate your display or make its colour accurate, including in Colour Work mode.
    • Visual comfort depends on more than colour: brightness, glare, text size, distance, how long you have been working, and the light in the room all matter too.

    For something evidence-based on sleep habits, the US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute is a reasonable place to start.

    It stays on your Mac

    — No account
    — No network requests, ever
    — No location permission
    — No analytics or telemetry
    — No screen contents or keystrokes
    — No camera, ever

    Settings live in one readable file you can export, import, or delete. History is off by default, and switching it off deletes what was kept. If you turn on app rules, Daylight reads the identifier of the frontmost application while deciding what to do and keeps no record of it.

    First launch takes one extra step

    Daylight is signed, but not notarised by Apple — that needs a paid Developer ID, and this build does not have one. macOS will refuse to open it on the first attempt. That is Gatekeeper doing its job, and you should be sceptical of any download that asks you to work around it.

    Two honest options:

    1. Open it once from the Finder. Unzip, then right-click Daylight.app → Open → Open. macOS remembers the decision. If it refuses outright, remove the download quarantine flag first:
      xattr -d com.apple.quarantine ~/Downloads/Daylight.app
    2. Build it yourself from the source below, which sidesteps the question entirely — it is one command and needs no Xcode.

    Build from source

    No Xcode required — Command Line Tools is enough. There are no third-party dependencies to fetch.

    ./Scripts/build-app.sh release && open build/Daylight.app

    Download the source (209 KB) — 263 tests included. Run them with swift build && ./.build/out/Products/Debug/daylight-tests.

    Two things that are not finished

    Multi-display behaviour has never run on real hardware. Every path is written and covered against simulated devices, but only one display was ever attached during development. Run Daylight --diagnose to see what your own displays report.

    External-monitor backlight control over DDC has never exchanged a byte with a monitor. Its packet framing is unit-tested; the hardware path is unproven. It is off by default.
